Chapter 1:
Understanding FCRA Section
1681s-2(b)
1. Overview of Section 1681s-2(b):
Section 1681s-2(b) of the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) is a crucial provision that
outlines the duties of furnishers of information (typically creditors or lenders) when
they receive a notice of dispute from a credit reporting agency (CRA). This section was
added to the FCRA to ensure that furnishers take responsibility for the accuracy and
completeness of the information they report to CRAs. Under this section, furnishers
must conduct investigations and respond to disputes in a timely manner, helping to
protect consumers from the negative consequences of inaccurate credit reporting.

2. Duties of furnishers of information upon receiving a dispute notice:
When a furnisher receives a notice of dispute from a CRA, Section 1681s-2(b)
mandates that the furnisher take several actions: a. Investigate the disputed
information: The furnisher must conduct a reasonable investigation into the dispute,
reviewing all relevant information provided by the CRA and the consumer. b. Report
the results of the investigation: After completing the investigation, the furnisher must
report its findings to the CRA. If the investigation reveals that the disputed information
is inaccurate, incomplete, or cannot be verified, the furnisher must modify, delete, or
permanently block the reporting of that information. c. Update all CRAs with the
corrected information: If the furnisher finds that the disputed information is indeed
inaccurate or incomplete, it must promptly notify all CRAs to which it reports, ensuring
that the corrected information is reflected across all credit reports. d. Provide notice of
dispute to downstream users: If the furnisher has previously provided the disputed
information to any other entities, such as secondary creditors or debt collectors, it
must inform them of the dispute and the results of the investigation.
3. Timeframes for investigating and responding to disputes:
Section 1681s-2(b) establishes clear timeframes for furnishers to investigate and
respond to disputes: a. Initial response: Upon receiving a notice of dispute from a CRA,
the furnisher must, within 5 business days, provide the CRA with a notice
acknowledging receipt of the dispute. b. Investigation and reporting: The furnisher
must complete its investigation and report the results to the CRA within 30 days of
receiving the dispute notice. In some cases, this timeframe may be extended to 45
days if the consumer provides additional relevant information during the initial 30-
day period. c. Prompt corrections: If the investigation reveals inaccuracies or
incomplete information, the furnisher must promptly make the necessary corrections
and notify all CRAs to which it reports.
Failure to comply with the requirements of Section 1681s-2(b) can result in liability for
furnishers under the FCRA. Consumers have the right to bring private actions against
furnishers who violate this section, seeking actual damages, statutory damages, and
attorney's fees. In addition, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) and

Chapter 2:
Identifying Inaccuracies
on Charged-Off Accounts
1. What is a charged-off account?
A charged-off account is a debt that a creditor has written off as a loss due to non-
payment. This typically occurs when a borrower has failed to make payments for a
significant period, usually 120 to 180 days, depending on the type of account. When
an account is charged off, the creditor closes the account and reports the charge-off
to the credit reporting agencies (CRAs). It's important to note that a charge-off does
not mean the debt is forgiven or no longer owed. The borrower is still legally
responsible for repaying the debt, and the creditor may continue to seek payment
through collections or by selling the debt to a third-party debt buyer. Charge-offs
remain on a consumer's credit report for up to seven years from the date of the first
delinquency that led to the charge-off, even if the debt is subsequently paid.

2. How inaccuracies on charged-off accounts affect credit scores:
Inaccuracies on charged-off accounts can have a significant negative impact on a
consumer's credit scores. This is because credit scoring models, such as FICO and
VantageScore, place a heavy emphasis on payment history and the presence of
derogatory marks like charge-offs. a. Lower credit scores: Inaccurate or incomplete
information on a charged-off account can result in lower credit scores than the
consumer would otherwise have. This can happen if the charged-off balance is
reported incorrectly, the charge-off date is inaccurate, or if the account is still being
reported as delinquent after the charge-off. b. Difficulty obtaining credit: Lower credit
scores resulting from inaccuracies on charged-off accounts can make it harder for
consumers to obtain new credit, such as loans or credit cards.
Lenders may view the consumer as a higher risk and either deny credit altogether or
offer less favorable terms, such as higher interest rates or lower credit limits. c. Higher
costs of credit: Even if a consumer can secure credit despite inaccuracies on charged-
off accounts, they may face higher costs in the form of increased interest rates, fees,
or required down payments. This can make borrowing more expensive and put a
strain on the consumer's financial
well-being. d. Reduced housing and employment opportunities: Credit scores are
often used by landlords and employers to evaluate potential tenants and job
candidates. Inaccuracies on charged-off accounts that lower a consumer's credit
scores may result in denied rental applications or missed job opportunities. e.
Extended negative impact: Because charge-offs remain on credit reports for up to
seven years, inaccuracies on these accounts can have a long-lasting negative impact
on a consumer's credit profile. This can hinder the consumer's ability to rebuild their
credit and achieve their financial goals.

Chapter 3:
what we look for
inaccurate reporting
1. Account status: This indicates whether the account is currently open, closed,
charged-off, or in collections. Inaccuracies may occur if the status is not
updated correctly or if it's reported as charged-off when it's actually settled or
paid.
2. Charge-off amount: The amount that was written off by the creditor as
uncollectible. Inaccuracies may occur if the amount is inflated or doesn't match
the actual unpaid balance at the time of charge-off.
3. Charge-off date: The date when the creditor determined the account to be
uncollectible and charged it off. Inaccuracies in this date can affect the timeline
for the account to be removed from the credit report.
4. Original creditor: The company that initially extended credit or issued the loan.
Inaccuracies may occur if the wrong creditor is listed or if the creditor's name
is incomplete or outdated.
5. Account number: The unique identifier assigned to the account by the creditor.
Inaccuracies can occur if the number is incomplete, incorrect, or belongs to a
different account.
6. Account type: The classification of the account, such as a credit card, auto
loan, or mortgage. Inaccuracies in the account type can affect credit scoring
and overall credit assessment.
7. Payment history: A record of monthly payments, including on-time, late, and
missed payments. Inaccuracies may occur if payments are not recorded
correctly or if the history is incomplete.
8. Last payment date: The date of the most recent payment made on the account
before it was charged off. Inaccuracies can affect the timeline for the account
to be removed from the credit report.

9. Date of first delinquency: The date when the account first became delinquent,
leading to the charge-off. This date is crucial as it determines when the
account will be removed from the credit report (usually 7 years from this date).
10. Collection information: Details about any collection agencies that have been
assigned to the account. Inaccuracies may occur if the collection agency
information is outdated or if the same debt is listed multiple times with
different agencies.
11. Balance history: A record of how the account balance changed over time.
Inaccuracies can occur if the balance history is incomplete or if it doesn't align
with the actual account activity.
12. Credit limit: The maximum amount that can be borrowed on a revolving
account. Inaccuracies in the credit limit can affect the credit utilization ratio
and credit scores.
13. Account opening date: The date when the account was originally opened.
Inaccuracies can affect the length of credit history and the credit score.
14. Responsibility: Indicates whether the account is individual, joint, or authorized
user. Inaccuracies can occur if the responsibility is not designated correctly.
15. Dispute history: A record of any previous disputes filed on the account.
Incomplete or missing dispute history can make it harder to track the progress
of resolving inaccuracies.
16. Late payment details: Information about the number of days a payment was
late (e.g., 30, 60, 90, or 120 days). Inaccuracies in late payment details can
affect credit scores and the overall assessment of creditworthiness.
17. High balance or highest balance: The highest amount ever owed on the
account. Inaccuracies can occur if the reported high balance doesn't match the
actual account history.
18. Payment status: Indicates whether the account is currently paid as agreed,
delinquent, or in default. Inaccuracies in payment status can significantly
impact credit scores and creditworthiness.
19. Remarks or comments: Additional notes or explanations related to the
account. Inaccuracies or misleading remarks can give an incorrect impression
of the account status or history.

20. Compliance condition codes: Codes used to indicate special circumstances
related to the account, such as a natural disaster or active military duty.
Inaccuracies in these codes can affect the interpretation of the account status.
21. Original loan amount: The initial amount borrowed for installment loans.
Inaccuracies can occur if the original loan amount is reported incorrectly.
22. Current balance: The amount currently owed on the account. Inaccuracies in

23. Scheduled payment amount: The amount due each month as per the original
loan agreement. Inaccuracies can give a misleading impression of the

24. Past due amount: The total amount of missed or late payments. Inaccuracies
in the past due amount can exaggerate the severity of the delinquency.
25. Payment rating or status: A code indicating the current payment status of the
account (e.g., current, 30 days late, 60 days late). Inaccuracies in payment
rating can negatively impact credit scores.
26. Narrative codes: Standardized codes used to convey specific information
about the account. Inaccurate or outdated narrative codes can misrepresent
the account status.
27. Special comment codes: Additional codes used to provide more detail about
the account status or history. Inaccuracies in these codes can lead to
misinterpretation of the account.
28. Estimated month and year that the item will be removed: The projected date
when the negative item will be removed from the credit report based on the
FCRA's reporting time limits. Inaccuracies can occur if this date is calculated
incorrectly.
29. Date reported: The most recent date when the creditor or collection agency
reported the account information to the credit bureau. Inaccuracies can occur
if the date reported is outdated or inconsistent with other account information.
30. Date updated: The date when the account information was last updated by
the creditor or collection agency. Inaccuracies in the date updated can make it
difficult to determine the freshness of the information.

Chapter 4:
real life examples
Three real-life examples of consumers who have successfully used Section 1681s-
2(b) to dispute inaccuracies on their credit reports and improve their credit scores:
Example 1: Sarah's Medical Debt Dispute
Sarah, a young professional, discovered a medical collection account on her credit
report that she believed was inaccurate. The account showed a balance of $1,500,
but Sarah was sure she had never received any medical services from the provider in
question. She sent a dispute letter to the credit reporting agency (CRA), which then
notified the furnisher of the information (the medical provider) of the dispute under
Section 1681s-2(b).
The medical provider investigated the claim and found that the debt belonged to
another individual with a similar name. Under Section 1681s-2(b), the provider was
required to notify the CRA of the error and request that the inaccurate information be
removed from Sarah's credit report. As a result, Sarah's credit score increased by 50
points, enabling her to qualify for a lower interest rate on a car loan.
Example 2: John's Student Loan Charge-Off
John had defaulted on his student loans several years ago, and the lender had
charged off the debt. However, when reviewing his credit report, John noticed that the
charged-off amount was incorrectly reported as $10,000 higher than the actual debt.
He disputed the error with the CRAs, which triggered the furnisher's obligation to
investigate under Section 1681s-2(b).
The student loan lender reviewed its records and confirmed that the reported charge-
off amount was indeed inaccurate. As required by Section 1681s-2(b), the lender
promptly notified the CRAs of the correct charge-off amount and updated John's
credit report.

Conclusion
In conclusion, understanding the various categories that may contain inaccuracies or
incomplete information on a charged-off account is essential for consumers who want
to maintain accurate credit reports. By carefully reviewing each category and
identifying any discrepancies, consumers can take action to dispute the inaccurate
information with the credit reporting agencies and the creditors. It's important to
remember that under the FCRA, credit reporting agencies and creditors have an
obligation to investigate and correct any inaccuracies that are brought to their
attention. Consumers should also keep in mind that disputing inaccurate information
on a charged-off account is just one step in the process of rebuilding credit. It's equally
important to develop and maintain healthy credit habits, such as making payments
on time, keeping credit utilization low, and avoiding unnecessary credit applications.
By combining the knowledge of these dispute categories with sound credit
management practices, consumers can work towards achieving and maintaining a
strong, accurate credit profile that will serve them well in their financial future.
